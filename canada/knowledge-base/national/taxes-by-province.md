## 1. What changes between provinces

- **Lowest overall deductions:** Nunavut, the Northwest Territories, Yukon, British Columbia and
  Alberta. Their average deduction rate is about 19–21% at $60,000 and 26–28% at $150,000.
- **Highest overall deductions:** Quebec and Nova Scotia (about 25% at $60,000 and 35% at
  $150,000), followed by PEI, Newfoundland and Labrador, New Brunswick and Manitoba.
- **The gap is material.** At $100,000 a single employee keeps about $76,700 in Nunavut, $75,400 in
  B.C., $74,200 in Ontario, $69,300 in Quebec and $68,900 in Nova Scotia. That is about $6,500 a
  year between B.C. and Nova Scotia.
- **Sales tax runs the other way for some.** Alberta and the three territories have only the 5% GST.
  The HST provinces charge 13% (Ontario), 14% (Nova Scotia) or 15% (NB, NL, PE). Quebec charges
  14.975% (GST + QST). B.C. and Manitoba charge 12% (GST + PST) and Saskatchewan 11%.
- **Cost of living, housing and rents usually outweigh these tax differences.** Read this alongside
  Agent 02's market records. This is my interpretation, not an official statement.

## 2. Federal tax and payroll contributions (all provinces)

Source: `nat-ca-federal-income-tax-and-payroll-2026` (CRA T4127 January 2026 and the CRA
current-year page).

| Taxable income | Federal rate |
|---|---|
| up to $58,523 | 14% (lowest rate cut from 15%; 14.5% blended in 2025) |
| $58,523 – $117,045 | 20.5% |
| $117,045 – $181,440 | 26% |
| $181,440 – $258,482 | 29% |
| over $258,482 | 33% |

- **Credits:** basic personal amount $16,452 (falls to $14,829 between $181,440 and $258,482 of net
  income) and Canada employment amount $1,501. Credits are worth 14%.
- **Canada Pension Plan (CPP):**
  - 5.95% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, maximum $4,230.45;
  - CPP2: 4% between $74,600 and $85,000, maximum $416;
  - the base 4.95% earns a credit; the enhanced portions are deductible.
  - The CPP base rate falls to 4.75% on 1 January 2027 (CRA, scheduled).
- **Employment Insurance (EI):** 1.63% up to $68,900 of insurable earnings, maximum $1,123.07.
- **Quebec instead:**
  - Quebec Pension Plan (QPP): 6.30%, maximum $4,479.30, plus QPP2;
  - EI: 1.30%, maximum $895.70;
  - Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP): 0.43%, maximum $442.90;
  - federal tax is reduced by the **16.5% Quebec abatement**.

**Mid-year 2026 changes applied for the full year:**

- **B.C.:** the lowest rate rose from 5.06% to 5.60%.
- **PEI:** a new 20% bracket applies above $200,000.
- **Newfoundland and Labrador:** the basic personal amount rose to $15,000 from 1 July 2026, giving
  $13,094 for 2026 as a whole.

Payroll used prorated figures in the second half of the year.

**Provincial health premiums and similar levies:**

- **Ontario Health Premium:** up to $900, collected through tax ($600 at $60,000 and $750 at
  $100,000).
- **Quebec:**
  - The Health Services Fund contribution has 2026 thresholds of $18,500 and $64,355. Its base was
    not verified, and it is not modelled here.
  - The public prescription-drug plan charges members an income-based premium. The amount was not
    captured.
- No other provincial health premium appears in CRA's 2026 payroll formulas as read.

**Data conflicts and gaps:**

- **Manitoba (disputed).** The CRA current-year page shows thresholds of $47,564 and $101,200. CRA's
  payroll formulas (T4127) and the Manitoba supplementary tables show $47,000 and $100,000 under
  Manitoba's indexation freeze since 2025. I use $47,000 / $100,000. The difference in tax is under
  $70 a year at any income.
- **Quebec rates.** Revenu Québec blocked both fetch attempts (HTTP 403). The thresholds and basic
  personal amount come from the Quebec Finance ministry's 2026 parameters. The four rates come from
  search extracts of Revenu Québec pages.

## 4. Worked take-home examples (estimate)

**Label: `estimate`** — record `nat-ca-take-home-pay-examples-2026`. These are not official
figures.

**Assumptions:**

- single person with no dependants, under 65;
- employment income only, from one employer, for the full year;
- no RRSP, union dues, childcare or other credits.

**Method:**

1. CPP/QPP = rate × (salary capped at $74,600 − $3,500).
2. CPP2/QPP2 = 4% × (salary capped at $85,000 − $74,600).
3. EI = rate × salary capped at $68,900. In Quebec, also add QPIP at 0.43%.
4. Taxable income = salary − (CPP × 1.00/5.95) − CPP2. In Quebec, use 1.00/6.30 for QPP.
5. Federal tax = brackets(taxable) − 14% × ($16,452 + $1,501 + CPP × 4.95/5.95 + EI [+ QPIP]).
   In Quebec, multiply the result by (1 − 16.5%).
6. Provincial tax = provincial brackets(taxable) − lowest provincial rate × (provincial basic
   personal amount + base CPP + EI).
   - Ontario: add the surtax and the Health Premium.
   - Quebec: provincial tax = brackets − 14% × $18,952 only. The deduction for workers (maximum
     $1,450) and any Quebec credit for contributions are *not* modelled, so the Quebec figure may
     understate net pay by roughly $200–$370.
7. Net pay = salary − federal tax − provincial tax − CPP/QPP − CPP2 − EI − QPIP.

## 5. Hidden costs and obligations newcomers often miss

- **Tax residence in the arrival year** (when it starts, how foreign income and assets are treated,
  and treaty relief) was not researched in this pass. It depends on the user's country (question 1)
  and is a Phase 2 item.
- **Quebec residents file a separate Quebec return**, because Revenu Québec administers Quebec income
  tax and CRA does not publish Quebec brackets. Those on the public drug plan also pay its premium.
- **Property taxes, land-transfer taxes and foreign-buyer taxes are provincial or municipal.** They
  are in Agent 02's lane.
